ROBOT BALANCE LAB

Can you keep it standing?

A standing body is like a pole balanced on a hand: it is always about to tip over, and only quick corrections keep it up. Try it yourself, then watch a simulated robot do the same job, then test your instinct. No maths needed.

What is this teaching?

The weight falls away from your hand: the further it leans, the faster it falls. Moving your hand under it is the only fix. A taller pole falls more slowly (a lean doubles about every 0.44 s on the 4 m pole, but every 0.22 s at a robot's 1 m), so it is easier to catch. A delay means your hand goes to where the pole was, not where it is now. A robot faces exactly the same two problems.

Level 2 · Push the robot

Now a robot does the balancing. Its feet stay planted; to stay up it shifts where its feet push on the floor. Choose how fast it reacts, how hard you push it and how big its feet are, then press Push.

Things to try
  • Keep the push at 0.5 and raise the delay from 50 ms to 150 ms. What changes?
  • At 150 ms, switch to large feet. Can it survive again?
  • Set the delay to 0 ms and push as hard as you can. Is a fast reaction always enough?
  • Try a tiny push with 250 ms delay. Watch the wobble grow.

SIMULATION · ILLUSTRATIVE · ALL PARAMETERS ASSUMED

The model is the textbook "weight on a stick" (linear inverted pendulum), seen from the front: a = (g / z) · (x − p). The body (x) accelerates away from where the feet push (p), faster when the centre of mass is lower (z).

Level 1: pole heights 4, 2 and 1 m; nudges at random times; the optional delay is 150 ms, added by the game on top of your own reaction. Levels 2–3: robot centre of mass 1.0 m high; feet reach ±10, ±15 or ±24 cm (outer foot edges), plus 6 cm of hip help; the robot reacts to the "capture point" with gain 1 and never steps. A generic shape, not any real robot or its controller, and not human motion data.
